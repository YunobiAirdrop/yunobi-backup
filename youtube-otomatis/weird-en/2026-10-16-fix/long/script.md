### beat-003
Number seven: Slow Down. May 19th, 1997. Deep in the waters east of Australia, an underwater microphone picked up something that lasted almost seven full minutes. The sound started high and slowly slid downward — like a giant exhale fading into the dark. Hence the name: Slow Down. The leading theory? Sea ice. Somewhere near Antarctica, an enormous sheet of ice may have ground against the seafloor — or an iceberg scraped past underwater rock. But here's the problem: nobody ever confirmed it. The sound was recorded once, never repeated, and its exact source is still just an educated guess.
**[ON SCREEN: massive Antarctic iceberg from underwater view, deep blue ice walls descending into darkness, tiny research hydrophone in foreground, documentary photo]**

### beat-004
Number six: Julia. March 1st, 1999. This one was so loud it was picked up by the entire Pacific hydrophone network — every listening station, all at once. It lasted only about fifteen seconds, but in those fifteen seconds it screamed across an entire ocean. The source was traced to somewhere between Antarctica's Bransfield Strait and Cape Adare. The best guess? A colossal iceberg running aground — millions of tons of ice slamming into the seafloor. But again: a guess. No one saw it happen. No expedition ever confirmed it. Julia remains a fifteen-second scream with no confirmed author.
**[ON SCREEN: colossal Antarctic iceberg calving at night, ice cliffs collapsing into black ocean, explosive white spray, dramatic documentary photo]**

### beat-005
Number five: Upsweep. This one has been haunting the Pacific since 1991 — and unlike the others, it never stopped. Every spring and autumn, underwater microphones pick up a strange repeating whistle that sweeps upward in pitch, over and over, for weeks. The sound changes with the seasons, which means whatever makes it is alive — or at least active on a seasonal cycle. Scientists suspect underwater volcanoes. The Pacific is ringed with them. But in more than thirty years of listening, no one has ever matched the sound to a specific volcano, a specific eruption, or anything else. It just comes back. Every year. Unclaimed.
**[ON SCREEN: underwater volcanic vent on the Pacific seafloor, black smoker chimneys with shimmering hot water, deep blue darkness, ROV lights, documentary photo]**

### beat-006
Number four: The Hum. And this one isn't in the ocean at all. It's in the air — and thousands of people hear it right now. From Bristol to Taos to Berlin, roughly two to four percent of the population reports a persistent low-frequency hum, usually at night. A deep drone, like a truck idling somewhere far away — except there's no truck. Sufferers have moved houses, soundproofed bedrooms, even fled cities trying to escape it. Theories range from industrial machinery to ocean waves to a rare form of tinnitus. In a few towns, a factory was eventually blamed. But in most cases? No source was ever found. The hum has no confirmed origin — and for the people who hear it, it never stops.
**[ON SCREEN: quiet suburban street at 2am, empty road under orange streetlights, one lit bedroom window, eerie stillness, cinematic documentary photo]**

### beat-007
Number three: the loneliest whale on Earth. Since 1989, Navy hydrophones in the North Pacific have been tracking a whale that sings at fifty-two hertz. Here's why that's strange: blue whales sing between ten and thirty-nine hertz. Fin whales around twenty. Fifty-two hertz belongs to no known species. For over thirty years, this whale has called out across the ocean — migrating, feeding, living a full whale life — and no other whale has ever been recorded answering. Is it a hybrid? A new species? A whale with a deformed voice, singing a song no one else can sing along to? It has never been seen. Only heard. Calling, year after year, into the dark.
**[ON SCREEN: lone whale silhouette in deep blue Pacific water, single animal against vast empty ocean, shafts of light from surface, melancholic documentary photo]**

### beat-008
Number two: sky trumpets. Starting around 2011, videos flooded the internet from Canada, Ukraine, Costa Rica, and a dozen other countries — all capturing the same impossible thing. A loud, metallic groaning from the sky. Like a giant trumpet. Like the earth itself clearing its throat. Witnesses described the ground vibrating. The sounds lasted minutes, then stopped as suddenly as they began. Explanations? Everything from meteors burning overhead, to military jets, to temperature inversions bending distant noise down from the upper atmosphere. But no single theory fits every case. And recordings keep surfacing — which means whatever it is, it's still happening.
**[ON SCREEN: dramatic stormy sky over a small town at dusk, dark churning clouds with strange light breaking through, tiny houses below, ominous cinematic documentary photo]**

### beat-009
And number one: the Bloop. Summer, 1997. The U.S. Navy's SOSUS network — a Cold War system of ultra-sensitive underwater microphones — picked up a sound so powerful it was detected on sensors more than three thousand miles apart. It was ultra-low frequency. It rose rapidly. And it was several times louder than the loudest known animal on the planet, the blue whale. For years, the leading speculation was biological — something alive down there, something enormous, something unknown. It took until 2005 before scientists settled on an explanation: icequakes. Massive Antarctic icebergs cracking and calving, they said. The sound of ice the size of cities breaking apart. Probably true. But think about this: for eight years, the smartest ocean scientists on Earth listened to the loudest sound the ocean ever made — and had no idea what it was.
**[ON SCREEN: split view, above: Antarctic ice shelf fracturing with giant cracks; below: dark ocean depths with sound waves rippling outward, infographic documentary style]**

### beat-011
BONUS: the music of the Moon. May 1969. Three Apollo 10 astronauts are orbiting the far side of the Moon — the side that never faces Earth — completely cut off from radio contact. And then they hear it. A strange, whistling music. An eerie, otherworldly sound with no explanation. The crew is so unsettled they debate whether to even tell Mission Control. The tapes stay classified for nearly forty years. When NASA finally releases them in 2008, the official explanation is almost disappointing: radio interference. The Lunar Module and Command Module radios, both left on, interfering with each other. But listen to the recording today — and you'll understand why three trained astronauts, alone on the far side of the Moon, weren't so sure.

### beat-012
Honorable mention: The Train. March 1997 — the same strange year that gave us the Bloop and Slow Down — hydrophones in the Ross Sea, Antarctica, picked up something that sounds exactly like a distant train horn. Long. Low. Mournful. In the middle of the ocean, thousands of miles from the nearest railway. The likely culprit? Another iceberg. One running aground on the seafloor, groaning under its own impossible weight. It never repeated. Like Slow Down, like Julia — a one-time performance. And that might be the strangest pattern of all. The ocean is full of sounds that happen exactly once: heard by machines, logged by scientists, and never heard again. Ghost frequencies. Part of the reason is simple — the deep ocean is the least explored place on Earth. We have mapped Mars in better detail than our own seafloor. Sending a ship to investigate a sound costs millions and takes months — and by the time anyone arrives, the ocean has gone quiet. So the sounds remain. Unexplained. Unclaimed. The planet, clearing its throat when no one is listening.
**[ON SCREEN: lone abandoned hydrophone sinking into dark deep ocean, faint ghostly sound ripples fading into blackness, melancholic documentary photo]**
