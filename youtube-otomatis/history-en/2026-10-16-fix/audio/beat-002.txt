Constantinople. The last living piece of the Roman Empire, eleven centuries after Rome itself fell. It sits at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, and it is wrapped in the most feared fortifications ever built: the Theodosian Walls. Three layers of stone, one behind the other. A moat sixty feet wide and thirty feet deep. Towers every hundred and eighty feet, packed with archers, crossbowmen, and the terrifying Greek fire — a flammable liquid the Byzantines guard like a state secret. In 1453, the city is a shadow of its old glory — maybe fifty thousand souls left inside walls built for ten times that number. And to defend all of it, Emperor Constantine the Eleventh can muster just seven thousand fighting men. Seven thousand. Against the largest army the region has ever seen. The odds are worse than ten to one. And the walls, for all their legend, have one weakness nobody talks about: gunpowder.
