That same day, Mehmed the Second rides through the broken gates to Hagia Sophia — the greatest church in Christendom, a thousand years old, its dome seeming to hang from heaven itself. He dismounts, kneels in the dust, and orders the church converted into a mosque. The city is his. It will become Istanbul, the glittering capital of an Ottoman Empire that will dominate three continents for nearly five hundred more years. The last Roman emperor lies dead in the rubble. And in the chaos, Greek scholars flee west carrying crates of ancient manuscripts — the preserved wisdom of the classical world. Historians say this flood of knowledge helped ignite the Renaissance in Italy. Think about that: one siege, fifty-three days, a twenty-one-year-old commander — and the world map, the world of art, the world of ideas, all redrawn at once.
