On the night of April twenty-second, the impossible happens. Mehmed orders his engineers to lay a road of wooden logs, greased with animal fat, over the hills behind the city — from the Bosphorus strait, up and over the ridge, down into the Golden Horn. Then, in the darkness, thousands of men and teams of oxen begin hauling warships across dry land. Seventy of them. Galley after galley, sliding on the greased rollers, sails furled, moving like some ghost fleet through the night. The defenders wake at dawn, look out over their "safe" harbor — and cannot believe their eyes. Ottoman warships are floating inside the Golden Horn. Behind the chain. The great iron barrier, their proudest naval defense, is now completely worthless. Byzantine sailors row out in panic to attack, and get cut to pieces. In a single night, a twenty-one-year-old has rewritten the rules of siege warfare. Military academies still teach this maneuver today — because nobody, before or since, has ever moved a navy over a mountain.
