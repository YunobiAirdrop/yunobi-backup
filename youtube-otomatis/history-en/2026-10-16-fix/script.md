## LONG SCRIPT — 10 beats (~60 detik/beat)

### BEAT 1 — Cold open (hook A)
It's one in the morning, May twenty-ninth, fourteen fifty-three. Outside the greatest walls on Earth, war drums are rolling. For a thousand years, these walls have never been broken. Armies from three continents have thrown themselves at this stone and died. Tonight, a twenty-one-year-old boy is about to do what no emperor, no khan, no crusader ever could. By sunrise, the last city of Rome will fall. And the Middle Ages will end with it. But to understand how a teenager broke the unbreakable... we have to go back fifty-three days.

[ON SCREEN: night siege, torches, massive triple walls]

### BEAT 2 — The city
Constantinople. The last living piece of the Roman Empire. For eleven centuries it has stood at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, wrapped in the most feared fortifications ever built: the Theodosian Walls. Three layers of stone. A moat sixty feet wide. Towers every hundred and eighty feet, packed with archers and Greek fire. In 1453, the city is a shadow of its old self — maybe fifty thousand people left inside the walls. And to defend all of it, Emperor Constantine the Eleventh can muster just seven thousand men. Seven thousand. Against what is coming.

[ON SCREEN: map, triple walls diagram, the Golden Horn chain]

### BEAT 3 — The boy sultan
What is coming is an army of eighty thousand, led by a boy. Mehmed the Second. Twenty-one years old. He has been sultan for two years, and the old viziers at his court still treat him like a child. He speaks six languages. He studies Alexander the Great the way other boys study games. And he is obsessed with one thing: the city that has humiliated every Muslim army for eight hundred years. His father tried and failed. His grandfather tried and failed. Mehmed has a plan neither of them had. It starts with a cannon so big, people laugh when they first see the drawings.

[ON SCREEN: young sultan portrait, army camp, maps]

### BEAT 4 — The monster cannon
The cannon is built by a Hungarian engineer named Orban. He first offered it to the Greeks in Constantinople. They couldn't afford him. So he walked to the Ottoman camp — and Mehmed gave him everything. The result is a bronze monster twenty-seven feet long. It fires stone balls weighing two hundred and seventy kilograms — heavier than three grown men — over a mile. It takes sixty oxen and two hundred men just to drag it to the walls. When it fires, the sound is heard ten miles away, and the crew has to run, because the thing might explode. Mehmed doesn't care. He has the biggest gun on Earth. And he is pointing it at the oldest wall on Earth.

[ON SCREEN: giant bombard, oxen teams, stone balls]

### BEAT 5 — The siege begins
April sixth, 1453. The bombardment starts. Day after day, the great cannon hammers the same stretch of wall. The defenders patch it every night with rubble, timber, and barrels of earth. It is brutal, grinding work — and it is working. The walls are holding. Then Mehmed tries the sea. His fleet sails into the Golden Horn, the city's harbor... and stops. The Byzantines have stretched a massive iron chain across the water. No ship can pass. The Ottoman navy — hundreds of vessels — sits useless outside the chain while the siege drags on. Week two. Week three. The walls still stand. The young sultan is running out of time, because a Christian relief fleet could arrive any day. He needs a miracle. So he invents one.

[ON SCREEN: bombardment, wall repairs, the great chain across harbor]

### BEAT 6 — Ships over the hill
On the night of April twenty-second, the impossible happens. Mehmed orders his men to grease a road of wooden logs over the hills behind the city — and drag seventy warships across dry land. Seventy ships. Hauled by oxen and thousands of men, up and over the ridge, down into the Golden Horn, behind the chain. The defenders wake up at dawn and cannot believe their eyes: an Ottoman fleet is floating inside their "safe" harbor. The chain — their greatest naval defense — is now worthless. Greek sailors row out in panic and get cut to pieces. In a single night, a twenty-one-year-old has rewritten the rules of siege warfare. Military schools still teach this trick today.

[ON SCREEN: ships hauled over greased logs at night, dawn reveal in harbor]

### BEAT 7 — The final weeks
Now the city is squeezed from land and sea. The bombardment never stops. The great cannon has cracked the outer wall in a dozen places. Inside, the mood turns desperate. Constantine the Eleventh walks the walls every night, talking to his men. On May twenty-sixth, Mehmed offers him a deal: surrender the city, keep your life, keep your riches, rule somewhere else. Constantine refuses. His answer is famous: the city is God's, and he will not give it away. He knows what is coming. On the night of May twenty-eighth, the churches fill with people praying. The emperor himself takes communion in Hagia Sophia. Then he rides out to the walls — for the last time.

[ON SCREEN: cracked walls, Hagia Sophia interior, emperor on walls at dusk]

### BEAT 8 — The fall
One AM, May twenty-ninth. The drums start. Wave after wave hits the walls — irregulars first, then the elite Janissaries. For hours the defenders hold. Then, disaster: a small postern gate, the Kerkoporta, is found unlocked. Ottoman soldiers pour through. At the same time, the great cannon finally blows a real breach in the outer wall, and the Janissaries charge through the gap. Constantine the Eleventh tears off his imperial cloak and charges into the fight with his men. He is never seen alive again. By sunrise, the flag of the sultan flies over the city. The thousand-year wall has fallen in fifty-three days.

[ON SCREEN: night assault, breach, fighting in the streets]

### BEAT 9 — Aftermath
That same day, Mehmed the Second rides through the broken gates to Hagia Sophia — the greatest church in Christendom, a thousand years old. He orders it converted into a mosque. The city is renamed. It will become Istanbul, the capital of an empire that will last nearly five hundred more years. The last Roman emperor is dead. The scholars fleeing the city carry ancient Greek manuscripts to Italy — and historians say this spark helped ignite the Renaissance. One siege. Fifty-three days. A twenty-one-year-old. And the world map is redrawn.

[ON SCREEN: Hagia Sophia, sultan entering, manuscripts, new maps]

### BEAT 10 — Payoff / why it matters
So why does this still matter? Because Constantinople proved something terrifying: no wall is forever. The cannon beat the stone — and from that day on, every fortress on Earth was obsolete. Armies stopped building taller walls and started building better guns. The Middle Ages ended not with a treaty, but with a breach. And it was done by a teenager the old generals laughed at. Remember that the next time someone tells you you're too young to change the world. A twenty-one-year-old moved seventy ships over a mountain — and ended an empire before breakfast.

[ON SCREEN: cannon vs wall montage, modern Istanbul, closing title card]
