But to understand how a dance could break a city, you have to see Strasbourg before the dancing started. Fifteen eighteen was a hard year to be poor. Two bad harvests in a row had sent grain prices climbing until bread — the one food everyone depended on — slipped out of reach for thousands of families. The market stalls of one of Europe's richest cities stood half empty. Syphilis, still new to the continent and still called the Great Pox, was moving through the crowded streets with no cure anywhere. And above it all rose the cathedral — the tallest building in Christendom — where preachers read every disaster as a sign of heaven's anger. Strasbourg wasn't just hungry and sick. It was braced for punishment. And frightened people are suggestible people. In the summer of fifteen eighteen, this city was terrified — of hunger, of disease, and of a wrath it believed was already on its way. A city like that doesn't need much to snap.

## NEW-B — Chapter: "The Paper Trail"
**INSERT after: old beat-005 (The Cure That Made It Worse)**

And here is the strangest part: you can watch the city's panic in paperwork. The Strasbourg council wrote everything down — daily minutes, ledger books, guildhall logs — and those pages still survive. Read them and the plague becomes an invoice: line items for hired musicians, entered like any city expense; the guildhalls officially cleared and set aside for the dancers; a wooden stage ordered and raised in the street. Payment after payment, approved in calm official language, as if the council were planning a festival instead of fighting a disaster. This is the archive the historian John Waller worked through to reconstruct those weeks — minute by minute, coin by coin. But read every page to the end and you'll find a gap that changes the whole story. The paperwork that records everything — the musicians, the permits, the daily decisions — never once says the word that matters most.

## NEW-C — Chapter: "The Saint They Feared"
**INSERT before: old beat-007 (Poison, Panic, or Saint Vitus?)**

But who was Saint Vitus — the saint whose name was on every frightened lip? He was as real as martyrs get: a Christian boy from Sicily, executed under the emperor Diocletian around the year three hundred for refusing to abandon his faith. The legends say the lions wouldn't touch him, and boiling oil wouldn't kill him. Over the centuries he rose to become one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers — the saints you prayed to when nothing else worked. And his specialty was dance. Medieval Europe believed Saint Vitus could strike sinners with an uncontrollable dancing curse — and, just as surely, that dancing at his shrine could lift it. His feast day was the fifteenth of June. His shrine at Saverne stood only a day's walk from Strasbourg. To a terrified city, this wasn't superstition. It was medicine, law, and weather all at once. If the saint was angry, the only question left was: who had angered him?

## NEW-D — Chapter: "1374: The First Dance"
**INSERT after: old beat-007 (Poison, Panic, or Saint Vitus?)**

And Strasbourg had seen the pattern before. In the summer of thirteen seventy-four — a hundred and forty-four years earlier — the Rhine itself seemed to dance. It began in Aachen: townspeople broke into wild, involuntary dancing in the streets. Then it moved — Cologne, Metz, town after town down the river, with new dancers joining everywhere it touched. Chronicles claim thousands took part. They danced for days, some for weeks, until priests led processions and performed exorcisms over their writhing bodies. By autumn the mania had reached the Low Countries and northern France — and then, spent, it slowly burned itself out. Nobody understood it then either. But everybody remembered it. The dances of thirteen seventy-four were written down, sung about, and passed from grandparents to grandchildren — until the people of fifteen eighteen carried the script in their bones. When the fear finally broke them, they didn't invent the dance. They inherited it.

## NEW-E — Chapter: "The Laughter Epidemic"
**INSERT after: NEW-D**

And if that sounds like a medieval fever dream, the twentieth century proved it can still happen. On the thirtieth of January, nineteen sixty-two, at a mission school in the village of Kashasha — in what is now Tanzania — three girls started laughing, and couldn't stop. The laughter spread from pupil to pupil until the school had to shut its doors. Then it moved to the next village, and the next: Nshamba, Ramashenye. More schools closed. Over the months that followed, hundreds of children and young people were affected — laughing, weeping, unable to concentrate, sometimes for days at a time. The doctors who investigated found no poison, no infection, no germ of any kind. Their diagnosis: mass hysteria, released by the pressures of a newly independent nation. The Tanganyika laughter epidemic lasted the better part of a year. Real people. A real epidemic. No dancing, no music, no fun. The same invisible machinery — fear, belief, and a crowd — moving bodies without permission.

## NEW-F — Chapter: "Why It Still Matters"
**INSERT after: old beat-008 (The Ending Nobody Expected), before: old beat-009 (Close)**

So why does a dancing plague from five hundred years ago still matter? Because the dance never really ended — it only changed costumes. Mass hysteria didn't die with the Middle Ages. It walked into a Tanzanian schoolyard in nineteen sixty-two, and it appears wherever fear travels faster than facts — in panicked crowds, in rumors that outrun the truth. The costumes change; the dance does not. The dancers of Strasbourg teach us something uncomfortable: a crowd can believe its way into real symptoms, and experts can believe their way into making it worse. That is the echo this channel is named for — not just strange events repeating across the centuries, but the same human wiring underneath them. Fear. Belief. A crowd. Five hundred years apart, the wiring hasn't changed. So the next time you see thousands of people gripped by something nobody can quite explain, ask yourself what the music is — and who prescribed it.
