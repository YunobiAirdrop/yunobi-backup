[0:30 — THE WOMAN IN THE STREET]
Strasbourg, summer of fifteen eighteen — one of the richest cities in the Holy Roman Empire, with a council that wrote everything down. That paperwork is the only reason we know this story. A woman steps into a narrow street and starts to dance. Alone, in silence. Later accounts call her Frau Troffea — but her name doesn't appear in print until nearly fifty years later, in the writings of the physician Paracelsus. We may never know who she really was. What the records agree on: she danced for four to six days, collapsing from exhaustion, then rising to dance again. No music played. Nobody cheered. Neighbors just watched from their windows as she spun past their doors, day after day.

Then it spread. Within a week, more than thirty people danced beside her. Dancers wept as they moved. They cried out and begged for help. Their feet bled through their shoes. They danced through day and night, collapsed, and rose to dance again. Now the city had to act. But instead of stopping the dance, the council's physicians blamed overheated blood — and the medieval cure for overheated blood was to sweat it out by dancing. So the council cleared the guildhalls, hired musicians, and built a wooden stage in the street. They turned a medical crisis into a festival. More music drew more dancers. More dancers needed more music. The treatment was feeding the disease — and for weeks, Strasbourg's official policy was simple: keep dancing. Weeks passed. The stage stayed up. [2:50 — DID THEY REALLY DANCE TO DEATH?]
So did people actually dance to death? For five hundred years, the terrifying number everyone quotes is fifteen deaths a day. But here's the problem: that figure comes from chronicles written decades later. The council's own minutes from that summer — the real paperwork of fifteen eighteen — never count a single death. The historian John Waller, who studied this case more than anyone, concluded people almost certainly did die — of exhaustion, heart failure, stroke — but the true number is lost. The legend says hundreds. The honest answer is: we don't know.

So what caused it? For a century, the favorite theory was poisoned rye — a fungus called ergot that grows on damp grain and causes convulsions. Elegant theory, one flaw: ergot doesn't make crowds dance in rhythm for weeks. It makes them sick, then it stops. The explanation most historians accept today is darker. John Waller argues this was mass hysteria, built from terror. Strasbourg had endured years of failed harvests, famine, and disease. Syphilis was spreading. And the people believed — truly believed — in the curse of Saint Vitus, the saint who punished sinners with an uncontrollable dance. The city already knew what a dancing curse looked like.
